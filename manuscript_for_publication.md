## The Chosen One
### A novel by Skyler Blue
### Recovered Edition — Version 2

*Loathing in the Woes of Ruthlessness: The Chosen One*

## A NOTE ON THIS RECOVERED EDITION

This is a newly reconstructed, complete-story Version 2, written from surviving story architecture, earlier excerpts, established chapter arcs and family themes after the long original manuscript became unavailable. It is **not** a verbatim recovery of the previous 122,377-word file, and its word count will differ. The fictional supporting characters have been renamed; **Kaylee, Luna Avigail, Summer Skye and Alexis Isabella-Jane retain their names**. The criminal network, the public officials described in the fictional plot and their actions are invented. No accusation about an actual person or government is intended.

The recurring four-note motif, chess knight, rainfall, compass and seashell are deliberate literary Easter eggs. Several imagined screens and services in SKYCOIN4444 echo the family theme, but none contains a child's real identifying information or a usable secret.

# FAMILY LETTERS — READ THIS FIRST

### For Luna Avigail, Summer Skye, and Alexis Isabella-Jane

Before you meet the man in these chapters—the nervous programmer, the poor decision-maker, the father who sometimes gets louder when he is afraid—I want you to know something the suspense can never change.

You are not responsible for this story. You do not owe it to me to believe everything I believed, choose my side in a grown-up disagreement, finish my projects, or spend your lives protecting my name. You get to become people I cannot yet imagine. That is the gift.

I remember three little voices learning to say Dad. I remember shoes left where shoes should not be, breakfast arguments about fairness, and the strange authority with which a child can declare that a rainy day is perfect. I remember how heavy a sleeping child feels when you lift her from a car seat, and how light the same child feels when you realize she will not always ask to be carried.

Luna: I have hidden knights in the pages ahead. A knight moves in a way that looks wrong until you understand the board. I hope you always know you can choose a different path, even when everyone expects a straight line. You never have to win a game to make me proud.

Summer: The rain returns here. Sometimes it's just weather. Sometimes it washes a chalk mark from the pavement and leaves the children laughing. You do not have to turn every storm into a warning. I hope you get a thousand afternoons when rain means only rain.

Alexis: You will find a compass and a seashell. One suggests direction; the other carries a whole ocean without asking the person holding it to understand the sea. You are allowed to be curious, lost, brave, uncertain, or entirely ordinary. Your worth stays the same.

If you reach a sad chapter, remember that a chapter is not a verdict. If you reach a frightening chapter, shut the book and have a good day. There is no quiz at the end of your childhood.

I am proud of you before you prove a single thing.

Love,
Dad

### For Kaylee

You were my first great love. I want this page to honor that without making you responsible for how the story ends.

We bought a house; we did not build one from the ground up. We built a life inside it, which was harder, funnier, and sometimes messier. We raised daughters who found comedy in the middle of our worst mornings. We tried. Sometimes I tried badly. I wanted the future so urgently that I failed to notice how much work the present asked from you.

There are things we will remember differently. You have every right to your own memory and to your own future. Love is not the power to tell somebody else what they must feel. I hope the girls remember not a courtroom of blame, but two parents who could learn to cooperate even after the plans changed.

The beginning was real. That will always matter to me.

### To the Judge, and to the Ordinary Years

I remember a difficult court process and the gratitude I felt afterward for the years in which I could work, study, volunteer, and be a father. My memory is not a legal transcript, and gratitude is not proof of details beyond the record. I would rather thank a person for treating responsibility seriously than dramatize a ruling that I cannot responsibly recount in full.

Some of the best things in those years were not big enough for a case file: showing up at school, carrying groceries, volunteering at a shelter, earning a paycheck, fixing a computer, making a daughter laugh with an extraordinarily bad joke.

The life I am grateful for was never guaranteed to be easy. It was enough to be real.

### For the Grandchildren I Hope to Meet

If I am there when you read this, ask me what I got wrong. I want to hear you laugh at how strange the old technology sounds. If I am not, I hope you learn more about your family from the ordinary stories told around a table than from the dangerous ones printed here.

The projects called Hope were never supposed to become your inheritance of obligations. They were my attempt to make useful things. You may improve them, ignore them, or build something I would never have thought of.

The one family rule I hope survives every version of every app is this:

**Proud before proof. Love before legacy. People before platforms.**

# PROLOGUE — THE WRONG LESSON

When the school auditorium went black, Skyler Blue was underneath the stage with a screwdriver between his teeth.

Above him, Romeo was dying.

"Blackout!" the stage manager whispered so fiercely that the headset crackled.

"Working on it," Skyler said.

"He's literally dying."

"Shakespeare can wait eight seconds."

The wrong spotlight flashed on, catching a plywood balcony with a paint stain shaped like Oklahoma. The audience laughed. Skyler pulled one cable, connected another, and at last the stage fell dark.

The crowd applauded. Somewhere overhead Juliet spoke a line about grief, and Skyler, sixteen and absolutely certain he understood tragedy, thought the whole problem could have been solved if two families had simply stopped interfering.

His teacher returned his essay two days later. He had written: *They died because the world wouldn't let them be together.*

Underneath it she had written: **What if they also died because nobody stopped to ask a better question?**

He disliked her answer. He disliked it so much that he kept the paper.

Years later, when a stranger offered him ten million dollars for his silence, that sentence would return.

By then he would have a broken family, a company assembled from half-finished miracles, a phone that sometimes frightened him, and three daughters whose names he refused to surrender to the people writing stories about his life.

But before the threat, before the four notes, before anybody called him the Chosen One, a girl in a church pantry handed him a cardboard box.

"Are you the computer guy?" she asked.

"Depends what broke."

"The freezer."

"Not that kind of computer guy."

"Then carry this."

Two cans escaped through a split in the box and rolled across the floor. She chased one; he caught the other beneath a folding chair.

"Expert," she said.

She was smiling.

Her name was Kaylee.

He would remember the warmth of that ordinary afternoon longer than any of the nights when a screen tried to tell him who he was.

# PART I — THE LIFE WORTH LOSING

## CHAPTER ONE — THE GIRL WHO WASN'T JULIET

Kaylee had two rules about Skyler's software ambitions.

He could dream as big as he liked, and he had to remember when the electricity bill was due.

"Hostile requirements," he told her.

"Adult requirements."

"Same thing."

On the floor between them lay the remains of a printer he had decided to repair while also developing a private messenger. A shoebox held screws. An extension cord crossed the doorway like a tripwire. On his laptop, a registration form glowed blue and offered users the opportunity to make an account that would forget them at the next restart.

Kaylee knelt beside him and pointed to a button.

"Delete," she read. "What happens if I press it?"

"The message disappears."

"From the screen?"

"Yes."

"From the database?"

He hesitated. "Not necessarily."

"Then it isn't deleted."

He hated that the best product review he'd received all week came from a woman holding a grocery receipt.

She put the paper beside his knee. "And the electricity?"

He closed his laptop.

They paid it together, squinting at an account number written on the back of an envelope. The process took four minutes. The conversation about how often she had to remind him took another forty.

Later he found her at the kitchen sink, staring through the dark window.

"I want to build something that matters," he said.

"I know."

"Something people need."

"So do I." She turned. "But I am living in the part that's already here."

He had a good speech prepared about the future. He had said pieces of it so often that it almost sounded true before he opened his mouth. Instead he dried a plate.

"Tell me what I missed today."

She did. It was a longer list than he expected.

They married their plans to a series of practical compromises: work shifts, classes, volunteer weekends, secondhand furniture, the kind of car that could be repaired with a sympathetic mechanic and a little luck. Skyler filled notebooks with names—ShadowChat, SKYCOIN4444, HopeAI, SkySchool, SkyHope—as though naming a future were the same as making it work.

At the pantry, somebody asked him to recover photographs from a water-damaged phone. Another person needed help applying for a job. A volunteer had forgotten a password.

Skyler learned that people rarely asked for an ecosystem. They asked whether he could help them today.

When Kaylee told him they were expecting Luna, he bought three parenting books and a screwdriver.

"Why the screwdriver?" she asked.

"Crib."

"We haven't bought a crib."

"We're going to."

She sat down laughing.

The hospital windows shook with rain when Luna Avigail arrived. Skyler had packed batteries, charging cables and a flashlight. Kaylee needed a hair tie. He went searching through three hospital stations to find one.

When he returned, flushed with the pride of an international negotiator, she held out her hand.

"Hero."

Hours later his daughter settled against his chest. A tiny fist opened against his shirt.

His phone buzzed with a reminder that a trial cloud server was expiring.

He silenced it.

"You can hold her closer," Kaylee whispered.

"I'm afraid I'll break something."

"You're not holding a computer."

He moved his arms and looked at the child sleeping there.

For the first time he understood that some things could not be saved by a backup, repaired by a patch, or replaced by a newer model.

He thought understanding it once would be enough.

It wouldn't.

## CHAPTER TWO — FIVE BORROWED YEARS

The courthouse air-conditioning was much too cold.

Skyler remembered that before he remembered what anybody said: the polished floor, a water fountain that clicked when it shut off, a man across the street struggling to remove a shopping bag from a bicycle wheel.

In this fictional past, an inquiry connected to a death had drawn frightened talk that outran the actual legal record. Skyler was young and sometimes repeated the most dramatic version of events himself. The process concluded without a criminal charge against him for that death. He came away grateful for the chance to continue his life and painfully aware of the difference between a rumor and a finding.

He called Kaylee from the courthouse steps.

"Can you come home?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Then come home. Luna needs a bath."

He almost laughed. There was no music cue, no camera moving upward toward the sky. Just a child who needed washing and a family that expected him for dinner.

Years later he would call the next stretch five borrowed years. It wasn't a legal term. It was his private description of gratitude.

The years had weather.

Summer Skye discovered rain and removed her shoes whenever she could. Kaylee kept a towel by the door. Skyler forgot to put it back. One afternoon Summer made him stand with her beneath the dripping porch roof and count the seconds between lightning and thunder.

"That means how far away?" she asked.

"Approximately," he said, and explained the math.

She shook her head. "It means we can stay a minute."

They stayed.

The years had games.

Luna developed a theory that the knight in chess was the smartest piece on the board because it refused to travel straight toward trouble.

"Protect your king," Skyler told her.

"Why doesn't he protect himself?"

He had no acceptable answer.

The years had a compass.

Alexis Isabella-Jane found a small brass one among the objects on his desk. She turned in a circle, watching the needle wobble.

"North is broken," she announced.

"Stop moving."

She sat cross-legged and watched until the needle steadied.

"Oh," she said.

Skyler would carry that little word with him.

They bought a house. They did not build it. The rooms were too small to keep a phone call private from a daughter determined to offer her opinion. Somebody was always missing a sweater, a shoe, or a school form. Sometimes Skyler was home to read the same book three nights in a row. Sometimes a shift ran late and he missed the performance he'd promised to attend.

Once he arrived after the school chairs had been folded away. Luna waited beside Kaylee near the exit.

"Did you see?" she asked.

"I got here as fast as I could."

"That's not what I asked."

He knelt. "No. I missed it."

That evening she sang the song again in her pajamas. He recorded the imperfect second performance, with Summer interrupting and Alexis shouting from the hallway.

He kept it.

Later, Kaylee sat beside him at the kitchen table.

"I don't need you to be famous," she said.

"I'm not trying to be famous."

"Then why do you talk like the whole world is waiting for your website?"

He looked at the laptop.

"I'm afraid I'll never make enough."

"Enough what?"

He couldn't say.

She took his hand and held it. The question stayed unanswered.

The next morning they made pancakes. One burned and became a map of Oklahoma, although none of them lived there yet. Luna claimed the handle of the pan was Texas. Summer said the pancake was a duck. Alexis ate the beak.

Nothing extraordinary happened.

Then Kaylee's mother died.

Grief entered the house without knocking and sat down in the empty chair at every meal.

## CHAPTER THREE — THE RICH MAN AT THE TABLE

Warren arrived carrying flowers too large for Kaylee's kitchen.

His car made the driveway seem smaller. Summer stared at it with an expression usually reserved for expensive amusement-park rides.

"Does it turn into a robot?"

"Only after six," Warren said.

She considered this a satisfactory answer. Skyler did not.

Warren was Kaylee's stepfather, a man with money, warm manners and the self-assurance of somebody who rarely needed to check his bank balance before offering help. He had come after her mother's funeral with photographs, condolences, an offer to cover repairs, and several opinions about how the family should manage the coming year.

"You could use some stability," he said over dinner.

"We have a plan," Skyler replied.

"Of course you do."

Warren smiled. Skyler heard an insult where perhaps there was only a joke. Kaylee heard practical help while she was too exhausted to keep solving problems alone.

"Not tonight," she said.

The girls were still at the table. Skyler lowered his eyes.

After they were asleep, Kaylee cornered him beside the refrigerator.

"My mother is dead," she said. "I cannot referee two men arguing over which one gets to take care of me."

"I didn't ask him to take over."

"He didn't ask you to disappear."

"I know what he thinks of my work."

"You know what you think he thinks."

The distinction annoyed Skyler because it sounded like one of his own technical arguments.

Warren had genuinely mourned Kaylee's mother. He also believed nearly every problem could be handled by hiring the right expert. An acquaintance introduced him to a private family-security consultancy promising to review online threats, monitor reputation risks and help protect children's information.

The form asked for addresses, emergency contacts and household routines. Warren checked boxes because the interface looked professional. He did not notice how generously it permitted onward sharing to subcontractors.

The vendor acknowledgment arrived the next morning.

**INTAKE APPROVED — SECONDARY ASSESSMENT QUEUED.**

Warren assumed that meant somebody would do the job.

A copy of the data went somewhere he hadn't intended. Later, what happened after that would matter far more than his dinner jokes.

Meanwhile Skyler was being offered another kind of help.

Vivienne Carrow, an executive several levels above him at the factory, had heard about his private software project. She asked him to describe it. He did, with the breathless excitement that had once charmed Kaylee.

Vivienne smiled.

"We encourage initiative."

"Good," he said.

"Send me the architecture."

"It's outside my employment agreement."

"Legal can decide that."

He went home uneasy and opened the contract he'd signed on his first day. The language was broader than he remembered. He requested clarification instead of forwarding his source code.

Two days later he was summoned for a performance review.

He wanted one large explanation for every unpleasant thing in his life. He didn't yet recognize the smaller, more dangerous possibility: several people making selfish or careless choices that intersected at exactly the wrong place.

## CHAPTER FOUR — LUNCH WITH QUINN

Quinn told Skyler the factory sandwich qualified as insulation.

He was examining it when she sat across from him and produced a packed lunch that contained recognizable vegetables.

"What are you working on now?" she asked.

"Messaging system."

"Still?"

"New part."

"Does it work?"

He showed her a screenshot. She clicked a button labeled DELETE.

"Does that actually erase the message?"

"From the interface."

"Then rename the button."

Quinn worked in quality control. She was quick with numbers, impatient with pretenses, and freshly divorced. She liked that Skyler could make a bleak shift funny. He liked that she asked questions about his work without rolling her eyes.

Their lunches became a habit. The habit became an escape. Skyler didn't tell Kaylee how much he looked forward to it.

He told himself the omission wasn't a lie.

At work, Vivienne's request for architecture notes turned into a formal risk review. Some complaints cited ordinary behavior: a late arrival, an argument on shift, a schedule he hadn't confirmed. Other allegations relied on unattributed reports about his personal contacts and outside projects.

Quinn read the warning letter.

"Some of this is nonsense," she said. "Some of it is stuff you did."

"Whose side are you on?"

"Whichever side can tell the difference."

That night Kaylee saw Quinn's name lighting up Skyler's phone after ten.

"Is there something happening?" she asked.

"No."

She watched him look away.

"Have you kissed her?"

"No."

"Have you wanted to?"

He opened his mouth, closed it, and finally said, "Sometimes."

Kaylee sat down.

"You let me think I was imagining something."

"Nothing happened."

"You just told me something happened in your head. I'm asking why I had to drag that truth out of you."

Skyler wanted a rule that would make him innocent. Kaylee wanted trust, which didn't fit neatly into rules.

A month later Skyler was dismissed. He disputed parts of the record and admitted others. The decision landed at home in the form of an empty lunch bag and a final paycheck that would not stretch as far as the bills.

Quinn waited near her car.

"I liked talking to you," she said. "That doesn't mean I agreed to be part of a secret."

She drove away.

At home Kaylee had arranged the bills in three stacks. She looked at the bag in his hand and pulled out a chair.

"Sit down."

He sat.

For an hour they made calls, postponed expenses and tried to decide where work might still be available.

Kaylee touched the pile nearest him.

"You know what scares me?"

"The money?"

"That you'll spend the next ten years proving how wrong they all were instead of deciding what we do next."

Skyler looked at a job listing in Oklahoma. He wanted a future that didn't include the humiliation of this one.

"Then let's decide," he said.

By morning the possibility of Sallisaw had become a plan.

## CHAPTER FIVE — OKLAHOMA, OKLAHOMA

The moving truck stuck in the driveway on its first attempt.

Skyler stood in Sallisaw directing the driver backward by inches while Kaylee held Alexis and tried to stop Summer from investigating a puddle the size of a small county. Luna wanted to know where the library was.

"Tell me this was part of the plan," Kaylee called.

"Almost all of it."

The truck roof scraped a branch.

"That part wasn't."

There were good days. The town had morning light that made the rooftops look forgiven. The girls learned a new route to school, new teachers' names, and where the stores kept the cereal they liked. Kaylee unpacked plates as if arranging them in a new kitchen might be an agreement with the future.

Luna came home singing an Oklahoma song.

Skyler was writing a pitch deck.

"Dad. Listen."

"One second."

She sang anyway. By the time he looked up she had finished the first verse.

"You missed it."

"I heard some."

"That's not listening."

He closed the laptop and invited her to sing again. Summer supplied an inaccurate chorus. Alexis beat time against a cup. Kaylee, washing dishes, laughed.

For a minute the move felt right.

Skyler registered IITR and opened fresh repositories for his growing SKYCOIN4444 ecosystem. ShadowChat would handle private messages. SkySchool would make difficult subjects learnable. HopeAI would offer patient explanations. SkyHope might one day make it easier for small charities to show where money went.

"How many of these are finished?" Kaylee asked.

"Define finished."

"Can I log in tomorrow and still exist?"

He made a note beside the database design.

**IF IT DOESN'T SAVE, IT DOESN'T WORK.**

The first ugly anonymous post arrived after an ordinary marital argument. It mixed public information with a private-looking detail and an insinuation about Kaylee. Skyler showed it to her as though she'd been caught doing something.

"Who wrote this?" she asked.

"That's what I'm asking you."

She went very still.

"Find out what you can actually find out. Don't ask me to confess to somebody else's caption."

He kept talking. That was his mistake. He could see the hurt on her face and still could not resist trying to win.

The separation came after more than one post, more than one argument and more than one exhausted attempt to begin again. Grief, money, missed promises and mutual fear had made their home increasingly difficult to share. There was no single villain that explained it.

Skyler sat alone the first night and wrote Kaylee an eleven-paragraph message. It tried to account for every job, every rumor, every unfair thing he thought he had suffered.

He deleted ten paragraphs.

*I'll be there for the girls at the time we agreed.*

She sent back the location.

Not a reconciliation. A plan.

For a contract job and a technical investigation, he rented a temporary room in another city. On his first night there, he unpacked the laptop and opened ShadowChat.

An email arrived through an old account-recovery address.

It had no subject and no sender name.

**CHECK YOUR PHONE.**

The device on the table lit before he touched it.
